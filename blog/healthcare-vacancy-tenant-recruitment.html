For Healthcare Listing Agents

From vacancy to healthcare tenant: build the case with market intelligence.

A vacant space should not be marketed to every healthcare concept. Use the trade area, service-line demand, and competitive landscape to identify the operators with a credible reason to consider the location.

By CurisData September 29, 2026

Vacancy-First Strategy

Do not wait for the right healthcare tenant to find the listing.

When a commercial real estate agent has a vacancy that could support a healthcare use, the usual marketing package answers the property questions: size, rent, access, visibility, and availability. A healthcare operator has another question: Why should this location be part of our growth plan?

A concept-specific gap analysis can help the agent answer it. Starting with the vacancy, CurisData can define a realistic trade area, examine the people and healthcare demand within it, evaluate relevant competition, and compare which service lines deserve targeted outreach.

Key Takeaway

The goal is not to declare that a market “needs healthcare.” It is to identify which specific concepts appear most aligned with the trade area, explain the evidence, and give each operator a focused reason to investigate the site.

Start with the vacancy, then define a defensible trade area.

A fixed-mile circle is a simple way to begin, but it can misrepresent the market around a vacancy. Roads, congestion, physical barriers, and the reasons patients travel can produce very different catchments from the same address. A drive-time analysis offers a more useful starting point when the assumptions are visible and applied consistently.

The trade area should also reflect the concepts being tested. Patients may approach a recurring therapy service, a referral-based specialist, and a walk-in clinic differently. One boundary does not automatically describe all three markets. CurisData can compare reasonable catchment scenarios so that the agent understands how the opportunity changes with the service line.

Residential population is only part of the picture for some concepts. Employment centers and commuter patterns may add context for urgent care or occupational health, while nearby family households and relevant age groups may be more useful when screening pediatric services. The Census Bureau’s OnTheMap application can inform where workers live and work, but workers should not simply be added to residents as though they were entirely separate potential patients.

Test specific concepts, not one broad healthcare category.

A market can look promising for one healthcare use and unremarkable for another. That is why the analysis should compare clearly defined concepts—such as urgent care, medical dermatology, pediatric therapy, primary care, physical therapy, or behavioral health—rather than produce one blended healthcare score.

For each concept, the agent and CurisData can identify the demographic characteristics that provide useful market context, the relevant demand category, the likely patient-access pattern, and the providers that represent direct competition. Those definitions matter. A diagnostic practice is not automatically a substitute for an ongoing therapy provider, and a cosmetic dermatology office should not be evaluated exactly like a medical dermatology practice.

Demographics help explain a market; they do not identify an individual’s health status or guarantee utilization. The analysis should use appropriately aggregated information and avoid treating age, income, family structure, or any other characteristic as proof that a particular person needs a service.

Make the gap analysis concept-specific.

A useful gap analysis compares estimated service-line demand with the competitive resources available to address it. The purpose is to find possible imbalances worth investigating, not to convert one ratio into a promise of patient volume.

  • Trade-area definition. Use a realistic access area around the vacancy and document the travel assumptions behind it.
  • Relevant population. Examine the demographic, household, growth, and employment characteristics that provide context for the specific concept.
  • CurisData demand. Review the applicable healthcare-demand estimate for the current market and, where useful, a forward-looking view.
  • Competitive landscape. Identify comparable services, validate distinct locations, and separate direct competitors from complementary providers or referral prospects.
  • Access signals. Investigate service availability, appointment access, hours, payer participation, or other operating details when reliable information can be obtained.
  • Property alignment. Retain the CRE fundamentals that matter to the intended use, including access, visibility, occupancy economics, and a preliminary fit with the operator’s requirements.

Provider records require particular care. CMS’s NPPES guidance describes provider files, updates, deactivated identifiers, and additional practice locations. A raw provider count can include records that do not represent distinct competing clinics, and it does not show whether an office is accepting patients or has available capacity.

Compare concepts before choosing whom to recruit.

The best output is a side-by-side opportunity screen. Instead of saying that the vacancy could be “great for medical,” the agent can see which concepts appear stronger, which evidence supports that view, and what still needs to be validated.

Imagine a hypothetical vacancy near growing family households, a concentration of relevant pediatric age groups, and few verified providers offering a particular recurring therapy service. That pattern could place a pediatric therapy concept on the recruiting shortlist. If the same site also has direct access to a large employment corridor, urgent care or occupational health might merit a separate comparison. Neither hypothesis wins by default; each needs its own demand, competition, access, and property-fit assessment.

The comparison should also show uncertainty. A promising demand-to-competition relationship may weaken if listed providers are missing from the source data, if a major system is already planning a location, or if the apparent gap falls outside an operator’s payer and referral strategy. Transparent assumptions make the analysis more credible, not less useful.

Turn the analysis into a targeted recruitment package.

Once the strongest concepts are identified, the listing agent can replace broad prospecting with a more focused campaign. The recruiting materials should explain why the address deserves attention from that particular operator, while making clear which conclusions are preliminary.

  • A mapped trade area with drive-time assumptions and a concise access narrative.
  • A concept-specific demographic profile showing only the market characteristics relevant to the recruiting thesis.
  • A demand summary with the service category, time horizon, geography, and modeling assumptions identified.
  • A competition map and inventory distinguishing direct competitors, complementary providers, and records that still require verification.
  • A gap or opportunity comparison showing how the targeted concept ranks against the other healthcare uses evaluated for the vacancy.
  • A transparent next-step list covering local validation, operator requirements, and open questions.

This gives the agent a different opening conversation: “We evaluated this trade area for your service line, and here is why the location may fit your expansion criteria.” The evidence can support outreach to regional operators, health systems, specialty groups, franchise development teams, or other qualified prospects without implying that the site guarantees visits, revenue, or leasing success.
