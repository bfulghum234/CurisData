For Commercial Real Estate Firms

Commercial real estate for specialty healthcare: beyond the square footage.

For autism services, dermatology, and urgent care clients, the right location starts with the right market—and a clear understanding of the service line.

By CurisData September 18, 2026

Healthcare Tenant Advisory

Your client needs more than an available suite.

For a commercial real estate company serving healthcare clients, the strongest property search begins before the first tour. An attractive address and competitive asking rent matter, but the larger question is whether the surrounding market aligns with the client’s services, intended patients, and growth strategy.

Brokers and tenant advisors can bring more value by connecting service-line strategy with location intelligence: who the clinic intends to serve, where those patients can realistically come from, what alternatives already exist, and how the location fits the operator’s broader network.

Key Takeaway

Do not treat “healthcare tenant” as a single market category. Define the service line first, then evaluate the patient catchment, relevant competition, referral ecosystem, and expansion fit.

Different Services, Different Questions

Three clients. Three different market strategies.

01

Autism services

Evaluate the intended age groups, referral pathways, recurring-visit travel, and availability of comparable diagnostic or therapy services.

02

Dermatology

Separate medical, surgical, and cosmetic service lines, then assess the patient market and competitive landscape for each.

03

Urgent care

Compare residential catchments, employment centers, convenient access, competing care options, and any employer-focused services.

Start with a service-line brief, not a listing list.

“We need a healthcare location” is not yet a market strategy. Ask the client to define the services offered, ages served, appointment or walk-in model, payer or payment approach, referral relationships, and purpose of the new location.

A first clinic needs a different conversation from a relocation that must retain existing patients or an expansion intended to reach a new catchment. Identify whether the goal is broader coverage, additional capacity, a new service line, or improved convenience for an established patient base.

Turn that brief into market-selection criteria. The question becomes, “Which areas best align with this operator’s offering?” rather than, “Which available spaces are already labeled medical?”

Define a patient catchment, not just a radius.

A circle around an address is a useful screen, but a service-line strategy needs a plausible view of patient access. Compare travel-time scenarios, road connections, and the locations of relevant population groups. Use consistent assumptions across candidate markets so the comparison means something.

A recurring therapy visit, a referral-based specialist appointment, and a walk-in urgent care visit can raise different travel questions. Let the operator’s experience and, where lawfully available, appropriately aggregated patient-origin data help test those assumptions. There is no universal travel threshold for all three.

Read population growth alongside age distribution, households, and employment geography where relevant. Keep source dates and modeled geographic allocations visible; a catchment estimate is not an exact count of future patients.

Autism services: map the service network and family journey.

An autism diagnostic practice, an ongoing therapy program, and a multidisciplinary clinic are not interchangeable service lines. Confirm what the operator provides and the age range it serves; do not assume every autism-related client serves only children or follows the same model.

For a pediatric-focused operator, examine the distribution of relevant age groups and family households, then investigate potential referral connections such as pediatric practices, developmental specialists, and school or community resources. Proximity identifies relationships worth exploring, not a guaranteed referral stream.

Map comparable services by what they actually offer. A diagnostic practice should not automatically be counted as a direct substitute for an ongoing therapy program. Where appropriately obtained, current information about availability, waitlists, and payer participation can help test a possible service gap; record the date and method rather than treating directory presence as available capacity.

For recurring services, compare the proposed location with family travel patterns and the operator’s existing locations. Ask whether a new site could improve convenience or extend coverage without simply drawing from the same patient base. Age-based population data helps frame the question; it does not establish autism prevalence, treatment eligibility, or likely enrollment.

Dermatology: assess each service line on its own terms.

Dermatology is not one uniform operating model. The American Academy of Dermatology’s overview describes care for skin, hair, and nail conditions as well as cosmetic concerns, and discusses specialties including Mohs surgery and pediatric dermatology.

For medical dermatology, compare the population profile, access to relevant practices, appointment availability where verifiable, and relationships with primary care or other referring providers. For a pediatric offering, evaluate the intended age groups rather than relying on the total population alone.

For a surgical or Mohs-focused offering, investigate the geographic reach of potential referring dermatologists and the availability of comparable services. A location near a healthcare cluster may deserve consideration, but the operator should validate actual referral relationships rather than assuming neighboring practices will supply patients.

For cosmetic services, investigate competing offerings, the intended customer segment, and the client’s own evidence about where patients come from. Household income can add context, but it does not establish willingness to purchase a procedure. A combined medical-and-cosmetic practice needs an assessment that reflects both markets, not one blended demand assumption.

Urgent care: connect convenient access with the care landscape.

Start with the operator’s planned offering: general walk-in care, pediatric services, employer-focused occupational health, or another defined mix. Each creates different questions about the population, competing services, and potential business relationships around a location.

Compare residential neighborhoods with employment centers and commuter connections. The Census Bureau’s OnTheMap application describes where workers live and work, providing employment-geography context for the search. It does not measure clinic demand or real-time foot traffic, and workers should not simply be added to residents as though they were distinct potential patients.

Map nearby urgent care centers and investigate other relevant care options, such as retail clinics and primary care practices offering same-day appointments. Compare verified service offerings, hours, access, and payer participation. A competing location may have a different role in the market; a low provider count alone does not establish an unmet need.

If occupational health is part of the strategy, examine the employer landscape and ask which services and relationships the operator intends to develop. Employer concentration is a screening signal, not evidence of a contract. At the property level, retain the essentials: visibility, straightforward arrival, and convenient access from the intended catchment.

Present a service-line location scorecard.

A useful shortlist explains why a market belongs on the list before explaining why a property does. Compare candidate locations using a consistent scorecard, with the operator setting the priorities and the supporting evidence visible.

Provider data can help describe the landscape, but the counting unit matters. CMS’s NPPES guidance describes provider files, updates, deactivated identifiers, and additional practice-location records. Validate distinct sites and relevant services instead of treating every record as a separate clinic or an available appointment.

  • Service-line fit. Identify the specific offering and the population or customer segment it is intended to serve.
  • Catchment and access. Compare relevant population characteristics, travel scenarios, and employment geography where useful.
  • Competitive context. Separate direct service substitutes from complementary providers, and distinguish listed presence from verified availability.
  • Referral and relationship potential. Identify relevant providers, community resources, or employers, with actual relationships distinguished from prospects.
  • Network expansion. Examine overlap with existing sites, potential new geographic reach, and patient-retention questions for relocations. Catchment overlap is not proof of cannibalization.
  • Evidence confidence. Show source dates, missing information, assumptions, and what the operator still needs to validate.

Then apply the CRE fundamentals: availability, occupancy economics, visibility, access, and lease fit. Property and clinical feasibility still need qualified review, but they should complement a clear market strategy rather than replace it.

Turn location intelligence into a stronger client recommendation.

Rather than delivering a list of addresses, give the client a clear explanation of which markets align with its services and why. A neighborhood might merit further investigation for recurring pediatric services, an employment corridor for an employer-focused urgent care offering, or a referral hub for a specialized dermatology service. These are hypotheses to test, not universal location rules.
