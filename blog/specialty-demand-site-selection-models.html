For Location Analytics Partners

Adding specialty demand to site-selection models: a dermatology example.

CurisData can give location intelligence firms a population-based view of specialty users and visits to evaluate alongside patient profiles, competition, access, network strategy, and observed performance.

By CurisData October 27, 2026

A Complementary Model Input

Add a specialty-utilization layer to the location model.

Site-selection firms already combine multiple views of a market. Demographics describe who lives nearby. Patient or customer profiles help identify likely users. Competition, accessibility, area draw, portfolio overlap, and operating performance help explain whether a particular site can succeed.

CurisData adds another lens: modeled annual users and visits generated by residents for specific physician specialties. For a dermatology assignment, that means estimating how the demographic composition of each market relates to dermatology utilization—then testing whether that information improves screening, scoring, forecasting, or client reporting.

Key Takeaway

CurisData is a demand input, not a finished site recommendation. Its contribution should be evaluated alongside the analytics firm’s proprietary data, modeling process, and real-world validation.

How specialty demand fits an established analytics workflow.

Healthcare location models often bring together geospatial data, patient or customer profiles, demographics, competition, area draw, accessibility, network strategy, and observed performance. Predictive models and whitespace analyses organize those inputs around a particular expansion question.

A location analytics firm may already maintain proprietary demand forecasts, client-specific patient profiles, or its own survey-derived utilization variables. CurisData can be assessed within that existing framework: as licensed third-party data, as an independent benchmark for another demand measure, or as an interpretable feature within a broader healthcare model.

The purpose is not to replace a partner’s methodology. It is to provide a consistent specialty-utilization layer that the firm can align to its trade areas, test against its own outcomes, and retain only when it adds decision value.

What the government survey foundation measures.

CurisData’s utilization foundation comes from a large-scale federal survey designed to support nationally representative estimates of healthcare use for the U.S. civilian, noninstitutionalized population. Its event data include office-based medical provider visits and hospital outpatient visits. Each event record represents a reported healthcare event, so a person with several visits can appear several times during the year.

The survey documentation identifies the physician specialty associated with applicable visits and supports linking healthcare events with person-level demographic characteristics. Dermatology is an explicit specialty category in both the office-based and hospital outpatient settings included in the model.

Because the source is a complex national survey, estimate construction must account for its weighting and sample design as well as sampling variability and the available specialty observations. CurisData uses the resulting utilization relationships with compatible local demographics rather than presenting raw survey records as local facts.

The current release models 25 physician specialty categories across U.S. block groups and reports two different measures:

  • Estimated annual users. Residents expected to have at least one included visit with the specialty during a year.
  • Estimated annual visits. Total included visits those residents are expected to generate, including repeat visits.

The current dataset uses a 2024 utilization reference informed by 2022–2024 survey records and ACS 2020–2024 demographic inputs. It is a modeled population baseline, not point-in-time observed utilization in 2026.

Build a dermatology demand layer at the trade-area level.

For each candidate site, the analytics firm can aggregate the block-group dermatology estimates to its chosen trade area. Counts can describe market volume, while compatible population-based rates can help distinguish a large market from a market whose demographic mix is associated with greater utilization intensity.

The user and visit measures answer related but different questions. Estimated users describe the breadth of the resident population expected to receive dermatology care. Estimated visits include repeat utilization and can provide context for the total annual service volume generated by those residents. The relationship between the two may be useful, but neither measure is a forecast of visits to a proposed clinic.

Trade-area construction should remain the responsibility of the location model. A firm might use drive times, empirically derived patient origins, distance decay, market-specific travel behavior, or another method. CurisData provides the demand attributed to resident geographies; the partner determines how those geographies contribute to each site and how overlapping candidate areas are handled.

Use the demand data as features, benchmarks, and explanatory evidence.

A location analytics firm does not need to choose one use case. The same CurisData measures can support different stages of the workflow, provided definitions remain consistent.

  • Whitespace screening. Identify areas with meaningful resident-generated dermatology demand before applying property availability and network constraints.
  • Candidate-site scoring. Add users, visits, rates, growth scenarios, or distance-weighted demand measures to a site score alongside competition and accessibility.
  • Supply-demand context. Compare resident-generated visits with verified dermatology locations or physicians. Treat the ratio as a screening signal, not proof of available market share.
  • Portfolio analysis. Estimate how much demand falls within a proposed site’s net-new reach versus areas already served by the client’s locations.
  • Forecast benchmarking. Compare CurisData totals with claims-based projections or proprietary market estimates and investigate material differences in definitions or coverage.
  • Client reporting. Use mapped users and visits to explain the population-demand component of a recommendation without exposing the full proprietary model.

For a platform that already derives a survey-based patient profile, CurisData may still provide value as a packaged national specialty-demand layer, an independent benchmark, or a way to reduce the work of maintaining survey ingestion, demographic application, geographic allocation, and quality context. Whether that value is incremental is an empirical question for the partner to test.

Keep the model architecture explicit.

A useful dermatology site model can separate four analytical layers rather than blending every signal into an opaque score.

  • Resident demand. CurisData dermatology users, visits, and compatible rates within the modeled catchment.
  • Competitive and referral context. Verified dermatology offerings, appointment access where available, primary care and related providers, health systems, and medical clusters.
  • Site and access characteristics. Travel time, visibility, co-tenancy, mobility, parking, property economics, and other criteria selected by the operator and analytics firm.
  • Observed business evidence. Client patient origins, claims, payer coverage, existing-clinic performance, referral patterns, and network overlap where lawfully available.

This separation makes the specialty-demand variable easier to interpret and reduces the risk of calling every favorable signal “demand.” It also helps detect double counting. Because CurisData estimates are shaped by demographics, a model that separately includes the same demographic variables should test for redundancy and excessive weighting.

Test whether the new layer improves the model.

Adding a plausible variable does not establish better predictive performance. The analytics partner can compare a baseline model with a version that includes CurisData dermatology measures, using existing locations or other observed outcomes when a suitable validation set is available.

The test should match the intended decision. A market-ranking model may be evaluated on its ability to order opportunities. A visit or revenue forecast may require error and calibration measures. A site-screening tool may focus on whether high-potential candidates are consistently advanced without allowing information from the validation set to leak into model development.

Review performance across market sizes, regions, clinic types, and data periods. A measure that helps nationally may contribute less in a narrow geography or for a highly specialized dermatology offering. Document transformations, trade-area rules, feature weights, survey-quality flags, and any instances in which the demand layer is excluded.

Protect the meaning of the numbers.

Several distinctions are essential when CurisData enters a site-selection model.

  • Resident-generated demand is not clinic volume. A trade area with 25,000 modeled dermatology visits does not offer all 25,000 visits to a new practice.
  • Utilization is not disease prevalence. Visits can include screening, evaluation, procedures, and follow-up; people with a condition may receive no dermatology visit during the year.
  • Demand does not follow facility walls. Residents may travel outside the area for care, while a clinic may attract patients who live outside the modeled trade area.
  • Competition is not included in the demand total. Provider capacity, appointment availability, payer participation, referrals, and future openings require separate evidence.
  • Fine geography does not create fine local certainty. Block-group values are modeled allocations from survey-based relationships, not direct local observations.
  • Overlapping areas cannot be summed. Counts should be aggregated across unique geographies, with portfolio overlap handled explicitly.

A practical partnership workflow.

CurisData can supply structured block-group specialty estimates and methodology context for a defined evaluation. The location analytics firm can then align the file to its geography, normalize the measures for its modeling approach, and compare the results with proprietary patient, site, competition, and performance data.

A pilot can begin with one specialty and a known portfolio. Dermatology offers a clear example: select a group of existing or candidate locations, reproduce the firm’s current trade areas, add CurisData users and visits, evaluate redundancy and incremental value, and review where the two analytical views agree or diverge. Only then should the variables move into production scoring or client-facing recommendations.
