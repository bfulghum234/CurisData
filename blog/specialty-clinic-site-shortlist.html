For Healthcare Tenant Advisors

From a long list to a shortlist: choosing sites for a specialty clinic.

When a specialty care client is ready to expand, the challenge is not finding available space. It is identifying which few locations deserve serious attention.

By CurisData September 24, 2026

Operator-First Site Selection

One specialty. Many properties. A more manageable decision.

A commercial real estate agent may know the specialty clinic, understand its expansion goal, and still face a market full of plausible vacancies. Each property can look reasonable on a listing sheet. Touring all of them consumes time without necessarily improving the decision.

CurisData can help the agent screen the long list through the client’s service-line strategy. By comparing the patient market, healthcare demand, competition, access, and network fit around each address, the team can focus its tours and deeper diligence on the most promising few sites.

Key Takeaway

Apply the same specialty-specific criteria to every candidate. The shortlist should explain why two or three sites rise above the others—and what still needs to be validated before selecting one.

Define what the additional location must accomplish.

Before ranking properties, turn the client relationship into a clear expansion brief. Specify the services offered, ages served, appointment model, payer or payment approach, referral relationships, and role of the new location. Is the clinic trying to reach an underserved part of the market, add capacity near current patients, follow a referral network, or enter a new growth corridor?

Expansion criteria should also reflect the operator’s existing footprint. A site can score well as a standalone market and still add limited value if its catchment substantially overlaps a current clinic. On the other hand, some overlap may be acceptable when the purpose is to relieve capacity, improve convenience, or retain patients. Catchment overlap is a question to investigate, not automatic proof of cannibalization.

This brief becomes the filter for the analysis. A pediatric autism therapy provider, a dermatology group adding a medical office, and an urgent care operator entering a new submarket should not receive the same site-ranking model.

Make the first cut with property and market screens.

Begin with the practical requirements the agent and operator already know: geographic search area, timing, occupancy economics, access, visibility, parking, and any preliminary property criteria. The purpose of this stage is to remove sites that clearly cannot support the assignment before investing in detailed market analysis.

Then map the remaining addresses. Several vacancies may sit close enough together to represent essentially the same patient market, while two similarly priced properties may reach very different populations because of road networks, congestion, or physical barriers. Seeing the candidate set geographically helps the team avoid treating every listing as a distinct market opportunity.

The result should be a reasonable analysis set—perhaps six, ten, or fifteen candidates depending on the assignment—not a predetermined winner. Availability can change, so preserve the criteria used to remove a property and retain alternates when the differences are small.

Build comparable trade areas around every candidate.

Each remaining address needs a consistently defined patient catchment. Drive-time scenarios generally provide more context than identical mile rings, but the assumptions should reflect the specialty. Patients may tolerate travel differently for recurring therapy, a referral-based specialist appointment, and unscheduled walk-in care.

Use the same methods across the candidate set so the comparison is meaningful. If one site is evaluated with a ten-minute drive time and another with a fifteen-minute drive time, there should be a documented service-line reason. Otherwise, the process risks favoring a property simply because it was given a larger market.

For concepts influenced by employment geography, the Census Bureau’s OnTheMap application can provide context about where workers live and work. That information does not measure clinic demand, and workers should not simply be added to residents as though they were entirely different people.

Score what matters to this specialty clinic.

A consistent scorecard makes a long list manageable, but it should not disguise judgment behind a single total. Show the underlying measures, weights, and confidence level so the client can understand why a site ranks well and adjust priorities when needed.

  • Relevant demographics. Compare the age groups, households, growth, income context, or other aggregated characteristics that help frame the specific service line.
  • CurisData demand. Evaluate the applicable healthcare-demand category for each trade area using a consistent geography and time horizon.
  • Competition and access. Identify providers offering comparable services, then distinguish directory presence from verified availability, capacity, or payer participation.
  • Referral and relationship context. Map complementary providers, health systems, community resources, or employers that the operator may want to investigate.
  • Network contribution. Compare new geographic reach, catchment overlap, proximity to current patients where lawfully and appropriately analyzed, and the intended role within the operator’s footprint.
  • Patient accessibility. Examine travel patterns, route convenience, and the practical ability of the intended population to reach the clinic.
  • Evidence quality. Show source dates, modeled inputs, missing information, and any local facts that still require confirmation.

Comparisons also require consistent data. The Census Bureau notes that American Community Survey values are estimates and publishes margins of error; it also advises care when comparing different estimate types, time periods, variables, and changing geographies. The same discipline applies across candidate sites: use comparable inputs and keep uncertainty visible.

Let the service line change the ranking.

A strong process can produce a different shortlist from the same properties when the specialty changes. That is a feature, not a flaw.

For a pediatric-focused autism service, the comparison may place greater emphasis on relevant age groups, family households, recurring-visit travel, referral pathways, and the specific mix of diagnostic and therapy providers. For dermatology, the model should distinguish medical, surgical, pediatric, and cosmetic offerings rather than treating every dermatology record as a direct substitute. For urgent care, residential access, employment geography, commuter routes, competing walk-in options, and the operator’s intended hours or employer services may carry more weight.

Provider counts need validation in every case. CMS’s NPPES guidance describes provider files, updates, deactivated identifiers, and additional practice locations. A record is not necessarily a distinct clinic, a matching service, or an available appointment. The analysis should compare verified services wherever practical and label unresolved records rather than creating false precision.

Advance the most promising few—with reasons, not just ranks.

The deliverable should narrow the field to a manageable set for tours, operator review, and deeper property diligence. For each finalist, provide a concise market narrative: the patient-market strengths, relevant demand, competitive position, contribution to the existing network, potential concerns, and the assumptions that shaped its ranking.

The top site on a numeric score may not be the final recommendation. A close second could offer materially better economics, availability, visibility, or operational fit. Another candidate may be the stronger long-term market but depend on an assumption that still needs local validation. Presenting the tradeoffs allows the operator and agent to combine market intelligence with real estate and operating judgment.

Keep one or two alternates visible. If a preferred property becomes unavailable or lease terms do not work, the client can return to a documented comparison rather than restarting the market search.

CurisData helps make the shortlist defensible.

CurisData’s data-source approach combines population, demographic, geographic, and healthcare-market context while distinguishing observed inputs from modeled insights. For a specialty-clinic expansion, the analysis can be structured around the client’s service line, existing network, target market, and actual candidate addresses.

The value is not a formula that chooses the property. It is a transparent process that helps the agent and healthcare client spend their time on the sites with the strongest market rationale, understand the tradeoffs between them, and move into tours and negotiations with better questions.
