For National Market Strategy

How much specialty capacity can a market support? Start with demand, not provider counts.

A national capacity assessment requires more than dividing residents by physicians. Build the analysis from specialty utilization, verified supply, realistic operating capacity, and patient access.

By CurisData October 20, 2026

A Capacity Framework

Demand, supply, and capacity are not the same measure.

A national healthcare screen often begins with a provider count. Markets with fewer physicians per resident appear undersupplied; markets with more appear saturated. That ratio can be useful, but it skips the most important parts of the question.

Different populations generate different levels of specialty utilization. Listed providers may work part time, practice at multiple addresses, focus on different services, or have no available appointments. A defensible capacity assessment needs separate measures for resident demand, provider supply, practical operating capacity, and accessible care.

Key Takeaway

CurisData estimates the specialty care residents are expected to use. It does not measure how many appointments local providers can deliver or how much volume a new clinic can capture.

Separate the Concepts

Four layers of a specialty-capacity assessment.

01

Resident demand

Estimated specialty users and visits generated by the people living within a defined geography.

02

Provider supply

Distinct physicians, practices, and locations offering services relevant to the specialty definition.

03

Practical capacity

The visits providers could deliver given staffing, schedules, productivity, service mix, and operating constraints.

Accessible capacity is the fourth layer.

Even when appointment capacity exists, it may not be equally accessible to every patient. Travel time, payer participation, referral requirements, language, appointment availability, service mix, age restrictions, and whether a practice accepts new patients can all affect usable access.

For that reason, an apparent numerical surplus of providers does not prove that residents can obtain timely care. An apparent shortage does not prove that a new clinic is financially viable. Capacity analysis should expose these distinctions rather than collapsing them into one definitive score.

Define the specialty before counting either side.

A broad physician category can contain materially different services. Dermatology may include medical, surgical, Mohs, pediatric, and cosmetic offerings. A national demand estimate for dermatology provides specialty-level context, but the corresponding supply inventory should identify which services each practice actually offers whenever that distinction matters to the decision.

The same rule applies across specialties. Align the demand measure, provider taxonomy, care setting, age range, and included services before calculating demand per provider or estimating headroom. A narrow supply definition paired with broad demand can create a false shortage; a broad supply definition paired with narrow demand can create a false surplus.

Establish a population-based demand baseline.

CurisData applies nationally representative government healthcare-utilization relationships to compatible local demographics. The resulting block-group estimates describe the annual residents expected to use each included physician specialty and the visits those residents are expected to generate.

For a national capacity study, aggregate counts across unique block groups into the market definitions selected for the project. Markets might be counties, metropolitan areas, custom service areas, or drive-time catchments. The geography should match the decision and expected patient travel rather than data convenience alone.

Use counts and compatible rates together. Counts describe the total utilization associated with market size and population mix. Rates help show whether the demographic composition is associated with greater or lower utilization intensity. Neither is a direct observation of care delivered within the market.

Build a verified supply inventory.

Public provider records are a starting point, not a finished count. CMS describes national provider files, weekly and monthly updates, deactivated identifiers, and additional practice-location records. A provider can be associated with multiple addresses, and a listed address does not establish current service availability.

The analytics team should resolve duplicate clinicians and locations, distinguish individuals from organizations, confirm relevant taxonomy or service offerings, and document the source date. Where practical, add practice websites, licensing information, health-system directories, local research, or other evidence to validate active locations.

Report both the raw source count and the analytical count after exclusions or consolidation. That audit trail makes it easier to understand why the final supply estimate differs from a directory search.

Translate supply into capacity scenarios.

A physician count becomes a capacity estimate only after operating assumptions are applied. Those assumptions may include clinical full-time equivalents, visits per clinical day, clinical days per year, provider type, service mix, appointment duration, no-shows, rooms, supporting staff, and ramp or vacancy factors.

Use ranges when direct operating data is unavailable. A low-capacity case might reflect fewer clinical days or a procedure-heavy mix; a higher case might reflect more clinicians, longer schedules, or greater visit throughput. Label benchmarks and management inputs separately so readers know which assumptions are observed and which are modeled.

For an existing multi-location practice, actual provider schedules and clinic encounters can improve the assumptions. For a national screen, capacity ranges are more honest than one universal productivity number.

Compare demand with capacity—then investigate the difference.

The initial comparison can be expressed as modeled visits minus estimated practical capacity, demand per clinical full-time equivalent, capacity utilization, or a range under different productivity assumptions. Each measure should be treated as a screening tool.

A Hypothetical Illustration

Suppose Market A generates 30,000 modeled specialty visits and its providers are estimated to supply 27,000 annual visits. The apparent 3,000-visit difference is not automatically available to a new clinic. Patient outflow, inbound patients, service mix, provider availability, and uncertainty in both estimates could materially change the conclusion.

Investigate markets in which the result is sensitive to small changes. A market that appears undersupplied only under the lowest capacity assumption belongs in a different tier from one that remains constrained across a broad range of assumptions.

Account for patient movement and overlapping markets.

CurisData demand follows residents wherever they receive care. Supply is associated with provider locations. The two measures therefore describe different sides of patient movement.

Claims, patient origins, referral data, or other observed flows can help show which markets export or import care. Without those inputs, the model should state that the difference between resident demand and local capacity may reflect travel rather than unmet demand.

National rankings also need consistent geography. Adjacent service areas can share providers and patients. Overlapping drive-time areas cannot be added without deduplicating the underlying population and demand. For portfolio planning, distinguish total catchment demand from the unique demand contributed by each location.

Turn a national analysis into an investigation queue.

A useful national deliverable does more than rank markets from best to worst. It groups markets by the strength and stability of the evidence.

  • Priority investigation. Favorable demand-capacity relationships that remain meaningful across multiple assumptions and data checks.
  • Conditional opportunity. Markets that appear promising but depend on provider verification, patient-flow evidence, or a narrow capacity scenario.
  • Balanced or well supplied. Markets in which current estimated capacity is broadly aligned with resident demand.
  • Uncertain. Markets with incomplete supply data, unstable small-area estimates, unusual travel patterns, or mismatched service definitions.

Each tier should identify the next question to answer. That makes the screen actionable without presenting modeled precision as certainty.

Use the capacity screen for strategy, diligence, and network planning.

An analytics firm can adapt the same foundation to several decisions:

  • Private-equity diligence. Test whether a multi-location practice’s existing footprint and expansion markets align with external demand and supply evidence.
  • De novo planning. Prioritize markets for deeper site selection, provider recruiting, and financial analysis.
  • Acquisition research. Identify markets that may warrant investigation without claiming that a particular practice is available or attractive.
  • Network optimization. Compare clinic overlap, market coverage, provider allocation, and potential capacity constraints across an existing organization.
  • Client communication. Explain how demand, supply, access, and operating assumptions contribute separately to a recommendation.
