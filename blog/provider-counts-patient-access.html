Market Evaluation

More providers do not always mean better access.

A provider map can help describe a market. Understanding whether patients can actually receive the right care requires another layer of investigation.

By CurisData September 3, 2026

Reading the Market

A pin on a map is not an available appointment.

A map with many healthcare providers can look well served. A map with few can look like an obvious opportunity. Both impressions deserve investigation before they become a location strategy.

The missing question is what those records represent. Are you counting clinicians, organizations, practice locations, or services? And does that count say anything about the experience of a patient trying to receive care?

Key Takeaway

Provider presence, service capacity, and patient access are different concepts. A directory can help establish the first; the others need additional evidence.

Three Separate Questions

Look beyond the provider total.

01

Presence

Which relevant providers or locations appear in the source, and how confidently can those records be connected to operating practices?

02

Capacity

What care can those practices provide, with what staffing, schedules, and availability? A record count does not answer these questions.

03

Access

Can the intended patients reach the service, use their coverage, and obtain an appointment that meets their needs?

Define the unit before counting it.

A clinician and the organization where that clinician works are not interchangeable units. Neither is automatically equivalent to a distinct clinic address. Combining records without defining the unit can make a market appear more crowded than the analysis supports.

CMS publishes provider information through the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System. Its data-dissemination guidance describes monthly files, weekly updates, deactivated identifiers, and reference files for additional practice locations. Those details matter when building a consistent view of a market.

For a site comparison, decide whether the analysis needs distinct clinicians, practice sites, or another unit. Document how shared addresses, multiple locations, missing information, and deactivated records are handled. Preserve the distinction between different records rather than assuming every row is a separate competitor.

Investigate the relevant service.

A general provider total can hide the question that matters to a particular project. For example, a physical therapy proposal needs an understanding of relevant therapy services, not simply every healthcare-related record in the surrounding area.

Use directory classifications as a starting point, then validate the services provided at the locations being compared. Public practice information, appropriate outreach, and local operating knowledge can help distinguish a plausible competitor from a record that is not relevant to the proposed service.

Keep the source date visible. A recently downloaded file does not, by itself, prove that every practice detail has been recently verified.

Availability needs its own evidence.

Consider a hypothetical market with several listed practices. The intended patient population might still face difficulties if the relevant services have limited hours, do not accept its coverage, or are inconvenient to reach. These are possibilities to investigate, not conclusions that follow from the directory.

The reverse also matters. A market with relatively few listed providers might have practices with substantial capacity, nearby alternatives beyond the chosen boundary, or patient demand that does not match the proposed service.

When collecting availability information, record the method and date. An advertised service, a phone inquiry, and an observed appointment sample are different forms of evidence. Avoid presenting a small or one-time check as a comprehensive view of the market.

Before calling a market underserved.

  • State what was counted. Identify the source, date, geography, service filters, and counting unit.
  • Review the records. Check address quality, location relationships, status, and whether the services are relevant.
  • Test practical access. Investigate travel, payer participation, operating hours, and appointment availability where appropriate.
  • Label the conclusion carefully. A possible service gap is a hypothesis to validate, not proof that a new location will succeed.

From Records to Reality

Count consistently. Investigate the patient experience.

02Validate service and availability
03Assess access in market context

A potential gap is a starting point.

This framework does not establish an official shortage designation, regulatory network adequacy, or guaranteed patient demand. Directory analysis should be supplemented with operational evidence and project-specific local due diligence.
